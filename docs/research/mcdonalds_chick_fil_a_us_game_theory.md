# McDonald's and Chick-fil-A in the United States: market evidence for a game theory study

**Research note, 12 October 2026.** “Chick-lie” in the request is interpreted as **Chick-fil-A**. The attached two-page Edgeworth duopoly exercise supplies the theoretical benchmark; it is not a source of observed restaurant data. All monetary figures below are in U.S. dollars (USD). Unless specified otherwise, a “2025” restaurant count is a year-end count and “2025 sales” cover the calendar year.

## Executive summary

McDonald's recorded approximately **USD 55.061 billion** of 2025 U.S. systemwide sales across **13,706** restaurants. QSR reports approximately **USD 23.918 billion** for Chick-fil-A across **3,287** U.S. restaurants. QSR's reported average sales per unit were **USD 4.088 million** and **USD 7.700 million**, respectively. Chick-fil-A's QSR row is marked as estimated; an industry report discussing its disclosure gives a more precise systemwide sales figure of USD 23,918,208,000. [S1–S3]

These statistics describe **realized revenue and restaurant networks**. They do not separately measure meals sold, demand at alternative prices, kitchen or drive-through capacity, or marginal cost. Thus they cannot by themselves calibrate the attached Edgeworth exercise or prove an equilibrium. A defensible empirical game would use nearby restaurants, a precisely defined lunch basket, repeated local prices, order volumes or visits, and throughput or wait-time observations.

### 1.3 Price evidence and what is missing

There is no defensible single national “McDonald's price” or “Chick-fil-A price.” McDonald's [McValue page][S4] says prices and offer participation vary; a [Chick-fil-A location page][S5] instructs customers to start an order to see that location's prices. Chick-fil-A also says its delivery menu prices are higher than in-restaurant prices and additional fees apply. [S6] A published deal price is consequently an **offer with conditions**, not a nationally comparable transaction price.

As sector context, the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reports that its **limited-service meals and snacks** consumer price index rose **3.3% over the 12 months ending December 2025**. This measures a broad category, not either chain's price change or the price of a fixed sandwich meal. [S7] McDonald's reports 2025 U.S. comparable sales growth of **2.1%**, benefiting from average-check growth; comparable sales combine prices, product mix, and transactions, so this is not a demand elasticity estimate. [S3]

### 1.4 Supply and operational availability

Restaurant count measures geographic supply presence, not the relevant short-run capacity `K` in the attached exercise. A lunch-period capacity would be the maximum number of the specified meals a particular store can reliably prepare and deliver during that period. Labor, fryers, assembly stations, drive-through lanes, order mix, and opening hours affect it. The public annual chain figures do not report this value. Moreover, [Chick-fil-A closes on Sundays][S8], making its supply effectively zero for a Sunday restaurant choice, while its high annual sales per unit are earned over fewer open days. Annual per-unit revenue should therefore not be interpreted as superior hourly capacity.

## 2. The attached Edgeworth exercise as a benchmark

The images set out a **symmetric, homogeneous-product, simultaneous-price** game with aggregate demand `Q(p) = max(100 − p, 0)`, common per-unit production cost `c = 10`, and capacity `K` for each firm. At equal prices, the firms split demand equally, subject to capacity. When one price is lower, the low-price firm sells first up to `K`; the high-price firm may sell the residual under the exercise's specified efficient-rationing rule. Profit is `(own price − 10) × own quantity`. The exercise asks the reader to show that when `30 < K < 45`, no **pure-strategy** Nash equilibrium exists. These `100`, `10`, and `K` values are **normalized teaching parameters**, not USD prices, observed U.S. demand, or measured restaurant capacity.

An explicit check at **illustrative** `K = 40` shows why the equal-price candidate fails:

```text
Candidate: both prices = 100 − 2K = 20.
Total demand at 20 = 80; each sells 40.
Each profit = (20 − 10) × 40 = 400 normalized payoff units.

Unilateral deviation: firm 1 raises its price to 21 while firm 2 stays at 20.
The lower-price rival sells its full capacity of 40.
Residual demand at 21 = 100 − 21 − 40 = 39.
Firm 1 profit after deviation = (21 − 10) × 39 = 429.
429 > 400, so the equal-price candidate is not a Nash equilibrium.
```

This is one worked illustration of the exercise's mechanism, **not** an estimate of how either restaurant chain would respond to a USD 1 increase. Other candidate prices must also be ruled out in a complete proof. The attached exercise makes that broader claim for `30 < K < 45` and notes a mixed-strategy equilibrium under its specified price range. The result relies on the demand curve, cost, symmetric capacities, rationing rule, and strategy space in the exercise.

## 3. Which game fits the chains better?

| Textbook assumption | U.S. restaurant reality | Study choice |
|---|---|---|
| Two firms are the entire market | Customers can choose many restaurants or other meals. | Define a **local competitive set** and record relevant alternatives. |
| One homogeneous good | Chicken and burger meals, brands, service, and convenience differ. | Use a narrowly matched chicken-sandwich basket; still model taste differences. |
| Common price | Prices and promotions vary by store, channel, time, and purchase conditions. | Record actual posted **pickup** prices at named stores on the same date. |
| Equal fixed capacity | Store size, staffing, equipment, and hours differ. | Measure separate lunch-period throughput limits and wait times. |
| Known common unit cost | Store-level marginal costs and rival information are not public. | Report sensitivity ranges; label any chosen cost as an assumption. |
| Simultaneous one-shot pricing | Stores can observe rivals, repeat decisions, and use temporary deals. | Specify a repeated weekly or monthly game if data support it. |
| Efficient rationing after one firm fills | Customers may wait, switch, abandon, or use another channel. | Measure queues, lost sales, and substitution if possible. |

For empirical work, a **differentiated price competition model** is a more plausible starting point. For example, a store's lunch demand can be written conceptually as `q_i = D_i(p_i, p_j, travel_i, wait_i, quality_i, promotion_i, other_options, time)`, with realized sales limited by `K_i`. The sign of the rival-price response is an empirical question: some customers may switch between the two chains, while many may not regard the meals as close substitutes. This expression defines variables; no coefficients are estimated from the national data in this note.

## 4. Proposed empirical study

### 4.1 Unit of observation and collection

Select multiple local areas containing both brands within a consistent travel-time radius. In each area, choose named restaurants and record the same **Monday–Saturday lunch window** over multiple weeks. Record Sundays separately as an availability comparison. Define one reproducible basket for each chain—for instance, a chicken sandwich, medium fries, and medium drink—with portion differences documented rather than assumed away.

| Variable | Operational definition | Publicly observable? |
|---|---|---|
| Own and rival basket prices | Pickup menu subtotal before tax; separately record coupon eligibility and effective promotional price | Often, via location-specific ordering pages or app; archive date and location |
| Quantity demanded | Orders or transactions for the defined basket, including attempted orders when capacity binds | Generally requires operator or commercial data |
| Realized sales | Completed transactions × effective prices for relevant items | Chain-level annual totals public; local basket totals generally private |
| Capacity | Maximum sustainable orders per lunch hour, by restaurant and channel | Must be measured or obtained from operations data |
| Congestion | Wait time, queue length, unavailable items, and order abandonment | Can be sampled, with measurement error |
| Rival set | Other nearby restaurants and travel times | Observable with a documented geographic rule |
| Controls | Day of week, holidays, weather, school calendar, local events, wage/input-price shocks | Usually obtainable from public or licensed sources |
| Marginal cost | Incremental food, packaging, and labor costs per additional order | Generally private; use transparent sensitivity analysis |

If transaction counts are unavailable, foot-traffic data can be an imperfect proxy, but it misses drive-through or delivery behavior and may have coverage bias. If only posted prices are available, the analysis can describe **pricing patterns and reaction timing**, but it cannot credibly estimate demand elasticity, capacity, profit, or Nash equilibrium.

### 4.2 Analysis sequence

1. **Describe** price distributions by brand, location, date, and channel. Report medians and ranges alongside sample sizes; avoid treating one location as nationally representative.
2. **Check substitution.** With transaction or visit data, examine whether an increase in one store's effective price is followed by changes in its own volume and a rival's volume within the same local market.
3. **Address confounding.** Prices may rise when demand is already strong or costs rise. Include store and date effects, holidays, promotions, weather, and rival availability. A correlation between two prices is not evidence of strategic response by itself.
4. **Check capacity separately.** Look for full queues, long waits, or stockouts at peak hours. High annual sales per unit alone do not show a binding lunch capacity constraint.
5. **Compare models.** Test whether observed prices and volumes are compatible with simple differentiated pricing and whether the Edgeworth capacity mechanism improves the explanation. Report sensitivity to market radius, basket definition, cost range, and capacity estimate.
6. **Interpret cautiously.** A best-response calculation is conditional on the estimated demand and cost model. Strategic pricing cannot be identified from two annual chain totals.

### 4.3 Hypotheses worth testing

- **H1, substitution:** A relative price increase at one brand reduces its local order volume and raises a nearby rival's volume, holding other factors fixed.
- **H2, capacity:** A rival-price increase shifts fewer customers to a store when that store is near its lunch throughput limit.
- **H3, differentiation:** Substitution is weaker between unmatched products than between tightly matched chicken-sandwich baskets.
- **H4, time availability:** Chick-fil-A's Sunday closure changes local alternatives, so Sunday observations should not be pooled uncritically with Monday–Saturday outcomes.

These are **testable propositions**, not findings established by the descriptive statistics above.

## 5. Limitations and assumptions register

| Item | Current status | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Chick-fil-A annual figures | QSR marks its table row as estimated. | Treat fine-grained ratios as approximate. |
| 2024 McDonald's unit count | QSR and the SEC filing differ by two units. | State the source and use a consistent series. |
| National sales | Observed money spent, across items and stores. | Do not label it “demand” or infer meal quantity. |
| Price | Location, channel, and promotion specific. | National sales divided by restaurants cannot identify a price. |
| Capacity | Not supplied by public chain tables. | Edgeworth `K` must remain illustrative until measured locally. |
| Cost and profit | Marginal costs and local profits are not comparable public disclosures. | Any profit-game calibration requires explicit assumptions or private data. |
| Annual average unit sales | Sensitive to restaurant mix, openings, and days open. | Do not infer queue capacity, productivity, or margins. |
| Two-player model | Many outside choices exist. | Results apply only to a defined local choice setting. |
| Causal inference | Price, promotions, demand shocks, and openings move together. | Simple before/after or cross-chain comparisons are not causal estimates. |
| Exercise's rationing rule | An imposed customer-allocation convention. | An equilibrium result may change with a different rationing rule. |
